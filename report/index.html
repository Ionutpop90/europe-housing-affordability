Locked out: Europe’s housing crisis is an access crisis

Since 2015, homes across the EU have become far more expensive than incomes can keep up with. Yet the share of people overburdened by housing costs has fallen. This report uses official Eurostat data to show why both are true, who is paying, and why there is no single European housing crisis: there are four distinct ones.

01 · The gap

House prices rose faster than incomes; rents rose slower than everything

Indexing four series to 2015 shows where the pressure built up. House prices pulled away from incomes until 2022, paused when interest rates rose, and have since resumed climbing. Adjusted for inflation, homes are more expensive than in 2015.

    Why rents look tame: the HICP rent index follows all existing tenancies, many of them regulated or long-running. New contracts in big cities have risen much faster, but Eurostat does not yet publish a harmonised series for them. Gaps in housing data are also acknowledged in the Commission’s 2025 European Affordable Housing Plan.

    03 · Who pays

    The burden fell for owners and stayed high for renters

    A household is overburdened when housing costs take more than 40% of its disposable income. The EU rate fell from to , largely because two in three Europeans own their home and mortgage costs stayed low for most of the period. The people outside that group tell a different story.

      04 · Young adults

      In the south and east, adulthood starts after 30

      05 · Supply

      Europe is permitting fewer homes just as demand peaks

      Residential building permits fell sharply after 2021, when construction and borrowing costs jumped. The Commission estimates the EU needs over two million new homes a year to 2035. Permits alone do not explain prices, though: the correlation across countries is weak, because supply responds to prices as much as it drives them.

        06 · Housing Stress Index

        07 · Four Europes

          Data

          • prc_hpi_q / prc_hpi_a: house price index, all dwellings
          • tipsho60: standardised house price-to-income ratio
          • ilc_di03: median equivalised net income (EU-SILC)
          • prc_hicp_ainr: HICP actual rents (CP041) and all items
          • ilc_lvho07a/c, ilc_lvho05a, ilc_lvho02: overburden, overcrowding, tenure
          • yth_demo_030: age of leaving the parental home
          • sts_cobp_a, demo_gind: residential permits, population

          Data quality handled

          • Currency break: Croatia’s income series switches from kuna to euro in 2023. Growth is taken from the continuous euro series wherever this happens.
          • Greece is not covered by Eurostat’s house price index. It is shown as n/a on price maps, and imputed with the median only in the clustering.
          • Outliers: index z-scores are winsorised at ±2.5 (Malta’s and Cyprus’s permit rates would otherwise dominate the supply pillar).

          Techniques

          • SQL window functions, arg_max for the latest value per country, FILTER pivots and UNPIVOT coverage checks
          • Composite index with leave-one-pillar-out sensitivity (Spearman)
          • k-means with silhouette selection (k = 2…6), validated against Ward clustering (ARI), and PCA for the 2-D view
          • Quantile classification for the choropleth; equal-area projection

          Limitations

          • Indicators have different latest years: prices to , survey data to 2025, price-to-income to 2024.
          • HICP rents under-represent new contracts, and national averages hide large city–region differences.
          • With n = 27, clusters describe structure; they are not statistical proof. Index weights are a choice, and the sensitivity test shows they do not drive the result.
          • The analysis is descriptive and shows correlation, not causation.
